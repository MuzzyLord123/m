More rooms are repainted because the white was wrong than because the workmanship was.
It is the hardest colour to choose and the one people spend the least time on.

## Every white is a colour

There is no neutral white in a tin. Each one is white with a small amount of something
else added, and that something is what you are really choosing.

- **Yellow or red undertones** read as warm. They soften a room and suit north-facing
  spaces that get cold, grey light.
- **Blue or green undertones** read as cool. They look crisp in bright rooms and turn
  faintly clinical in dark ones.
- **Grey or black undertones** knock the brightness back. Useful in a room with a lot
  of glass, harsh in a room without.

Hold three whites next to each other on a sheet of paper and the undertones become
obvious. Hold one on its own and it just looks white, which is exactly why choosing
from a single card goes wrong.

## Which way does the room face

This matters more than the colour card.

**North-facing rooms** get indirect, cool, blue-grey light all day. A cool white in
here goes flat and slightly grey. Pick something with a touch of warmth in it.

**South-facing rooms** get strong warm light for most of the day. They can carry a
cooler white easily, and a warm white in strong sun can look creamy or yellow by
mid-afternoon.

**East-facing rooms** are warm in the morning and cool by evening. **West-facing** is
the reverse. Both are more forgiving, but look at samples at the time of day you
actually use the room.

<PullQuote>
  Pick the white for the light the room gets, not for the white you liked in someone
  else's house.
</PullQuote>

## Test properly, or do not bother

Painting a patch straight onto the wall is the most common way to test and the least
reliable. The old colour bleeds through, and the patch is surrounded by the very
colour you are trying to replace.

Do it this way instead:

1. Get sample pots of no more than three whites. More than three and you will go round
   in circles.
2. Paint two coats onto A4 sheets of white primed card, not straight onto the wall.
3. Leave them a full day to dry. Emulsion looks different wet, and most whites dry
   slightly darker than they go on.
4. Move each card around the room over a whole day: by the window, on the darkest
   wall, next to the skirting, next to the flooring and the curtains.
5. Look again after dark with the lights on. Warm LED bulbs push a white noticeably
   yellow, and cool bulbs drain it.

The card that still looks right in all of those places is your white. It is rarely the
one that looked best in the shop.

## Walls and ceiling do not have to match

A common instinct is to use the same white everywhere. Two adjustments usually work
better.

Use a **flat brilliant white on the ceiling** and your chosen white on the walls. The
ceiling then reads as light rather than as a colour, and the walls keep their
character.

On woodwork, matching the wall white exactly can look unfinished. Either take the same
white in a satin or eggshell finish, so the sheen does the separating, or go a shade
crisper for a deliberate edge.

## Finishes, briefly

- **Matt** on walls and ceilings. It hides imperfection better than anything else,
  which matters most on old plaster.
- **Washable matt** in kitchens, halls and anywhere with children. It costs more per
  tin and pays for itself the first time something is wiped off.
- **Satin or eggshell** on woodwork. Water-based stays white; traditional oil-based
  gloss yellows within two years, particularly on anything out of direct light.
